Problem solving in engineering

Real engineering problems don’t come with a formula at the top. Learn the habits that turn an open, messy problem into a safe, economical solution – then test yourself.

Start the practice quiz Worked examples
01

What is engineering problem solving?

In class, most exercises give you exactly the data you need and one right answer. Engineering practice is different – and that difference is where higher-order thinking lives.

A textbook exercise

  • All the data is given, nothing extra
  • The method is obvious from the chapter
  • One correct answer
  • Success = getting the number right

A real engineering problem

  • Data is missing, uncertain or contradictory
  • You choose the model and the method
  • Several workable solutions, each with trade-offs
  • Success = safe, economical, buildable – and justified

The eight key skills

Strong problem solvers use these habits every time. Each one sits at a higher-order thinking level: analyse, evaluate or create.

03

The problem-solving cycle

The eight skills fit into one repeating cycle. When a check fails, you go round again – that’s not failure, it’s how engineering works.

  1. DefineWhat is actually being asked? What are the constraints?
  2. AnalyseBreak the problem down and model it with diagrams.
  3. GenerateList more than one way to solve it.
  4. Select & solvePick the best option and work it through.
  5. CheckUnits, estimates, code limits – does it make sense?
  6. ReflectWhat would you do differently next time?

Sizing a reinforced-concrete beam

The problem

A simply supported reinforced-concrete beam spans 6 m in an office building. It carries a service dead load of 20 kN/m (including self-weight) and a live load of 12 kN/m. The architect allows a beam depth of no more than 550 mm. Materials: \(f'_c = 28\ \text{MPa}\), \(f_y = 420\ \text{MPa}\). Design the beam for bending.

Step 1Define

Knowns: span, loads, materials, depth limit. Unknowns: beam size \(b \times h\) and tension steel \(A_s\). Constraint: \(h \le 550\ \text{mm}\). Goal: \(\phi M_n \ge M_u\).

Step 2Model

Simply supported beam with a uniform load. Factor the loads, then find the maximum moment at midspan.

\[\begin{aligned} w_u &= 1.2D + 1.6L = 1.2(20) + 1.6(12) = 43.2\ \text{kN/m}\\[4pt] M_u &= \frac{w_u L^2}{8} = \frac{43.2 \times 6^2}{8} = 194.4\ \text{kN·m} \end{aligned}\]
Step 3Estimate

ACI gives a minimum depth for a simply supported beam, so a 500 mm deep beam (\(d \approx 440\) mm) is reasonable. A rough steel area follows from taking the lever arm as about \(0.9d\):

\[\begin{aligned} h_{\min} &= \frac{L}{16} = \frac{6000}{16} = 375\ \text{mm}\\[4pt] A_s &\approx \frac{M_u}{\phi f_y\,(0.9d)} = \frac{194.4 \times 10^6}{0.9 \times 420 \times 0.9 \times 440} \approx 1300\ \text{mm}^2 \end{aligned}\]
Step 4Generate

Option A: 300 × 500 mm with about 1300 mm² of steel. Option B: 250 × 650 mm – less steel, but breaks the 550 mm limit. Option C: a steel I-beam – shallower, but a different system and fire protection needed.

Step 5Select & solve

Option A meets the depth limit with ordinary detailing. With \(b = 300\) mm and \(d = 440\) mm:

\[\begin{aligned} R_n &= \frac{M_u}{\phi\, b\, d^2} = \frac{194.4 \times 10^6}{0.9 \times 300 \times 440^2} = 3.72\ \text{MPa}\\[6pt] \rho &= \frac{0.85 f'_c}{f_y}\left[1 - \sqrt{1 - \frac{2R_n}{0.85 f'_c}}\,\right] = 0.0097\\[6pt] A_s &= \rho\, b\, d = 0.0097 \times 300 \times 440 \approx 1278\ \text{mm}^2 \end{aligned}\]

Use 3 Ø25 bars: \(A_s = 1473\ \text{mm}^2\).

Step 6Check
\[\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{A_s f_y}{0.85 f'_c\, b} = \frac{1473 \times 420}{0.85 \times 28 \times 300} = 86.6\ \text{mm}\\[6pt] c &= \frac{a}{\beta_1} = \frac{86.6}{0.85} = 101.9\ \text{mm}\\[6pt] \varepsilon_t &= 0.003\,\frac{d - c}{c} = 0.0100 > 0.005 \;\Rightarrow\; \phi = 0.90 \ \checkmark\\[6pt] \phi M_n &= \phi A_s f_y \left(d - \frac{a}{2}\right) = 0.9 \times 1473 \times 420 \times \left(440 - \frac{86.6}{2}\right) = 220.9\ \text{kN·m} \;\ge\; M_u \ \checkmark\\[6pt] A_{s,\min} &= \frac{1.4}{f_y}\, b\, d = 440\ \text{mm}^2 \;<\; 1473\ \text{mm}^2 \ \checkmark \end{aligned}\]

The detailed answer (1278 mm²) is close to the estimate (≈1300 mm²) – a good sign there’s no arithmetic slip.

Step 7Reflect

The section has about 14% spare capacity. Could 2 Ø25 + 1 Ø20 (\(A_s = 1296\ \text{mm}^2\)) do the job with less steel? What would change if the live load doubled? Asking these questions is how you get better at the next design.

Simplified teaching example to ACI 318 (SI units): flexure only. A full design would also check shear, deflection, crack control and bar spacing.

Critical path and schedule crashing

The problem

A small building project has six activities. Find the critical path and the project duration. The client then asks for the project to finish 2 days earlier. What is the cheapest way to achieve this?

Step 1Define

Knowns: durations, logic, crash costs and limits. Unknowns: critical path, project duration \(T\), floats, cheapest crash plan. Constraint: reduce \(T\) by 2 days. Goal: minimum extra cost.

Step 2Model

Draw the activity-on-node network from the predecessor list.

Filled boxes = the critical path, found in Step 3.

Step 3Analyse

Forward pass for early times, backward pass for late times, then the total float of each activity:

\[\begin{aligned} ES_j &= \max_i\left(EF_i\right), &\quad EF_j &= ES_j + t_j\\[4pt] LF_i &= \min_j\left(LS_j\right), &\quad LS_i &= LF_i - t_i\\[4pt] TF &= LS - ES = LF - EF \end{aligned}\]

A03030
B37370
C35794
D7127120
E589124
F121412140

Critical path: A – B – D – F, with \(T = 3 + 4 + 5 + 2 = 14\) days. The other path, A – C – E – F, takes 10 days and has 4 days of float.

Step 4Estimate

Only critical activities can shorten the project. Because the non-critical path has 4 days of float, a 2-day reduction will not create a second critical path – so we only need to look at B, D and F.

Step 5Generate

Possible ways to save 2 days on the critical path:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{D} \times 2 &: \quad 2 \times 2000 = 4000\ \text{AED}\\[4pt] \text{B} + \text{D} &: \quad 3000 + 2000 = 5000\ \text{AED}\\[4pt] \text{D} + \text{F} &: \quad 2000 + 4000 = 6000\ \text{AED}\\[4pt] \text{B} + \text{F} &: \quad 3000 + 4000 = 7000\ \text{AED} \end{aligned}\]
Step 6Select & solve

Crash activity D by 2 days – the cheapest option, and within D’s 2-day limit.

\[\Delta C = 2 \times 2000 = 4000\ \text{AED}, \qquad T_{\text{new}} = 14 - 2 = 12\ \text{days}\]
Step 7Check
\[\begin{aligned} \text{A–B–D–F} &= 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 12\ \text{days}\\[4pt] \text{A–C–E–F} &= 3 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 10\ \text{days} \;\le\; 12 \ \checkmark \end{aligned}\]

A – B – D – F is still the only critical path, and C and E now have 2 days of float.

Step 8Reflect

What if the client wanted 5 days? After D is fully crashed, every extra day saved on A – B – D – F would also need to be found on A – C – E – F once its float runs out. Crashing then gets more expensive with each day – the classic time–cost trade-off.

Simplified teaching example: deterministic durations, linear crash costs and finish-to-start links only.
